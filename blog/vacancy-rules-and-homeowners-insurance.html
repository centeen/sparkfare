Vacancy Rules and Your Homeowner Insurance: What to Ask Before You Leave

Away Mode · Last reviewed 2026-11-01

Many homeowners assume that property insurance remains identical whether they are sleeping in their master bedroom or travelling abroad for two months. In practice, some policies treat an extended absence differently. Whether yours does is in your policy or a short call away. This page is general education, not advice. We cannot tell you what your policy says, and it does not assess your home.

Here are some terms insurers use and the questions to ask your insurance agent before you go.

Key Definitions: "Unoccupied" vs. "Vacant"

Insurers use these words, and the definitions vary by insurer and by state. Check the wording in your own policy:

  • Unoccupied: A home that is fully furnished, has active utility services, and contains personal property, but where the residents are temporarily absent with the intention of returning. A home left empty while you take a four-week or eight-week trip may be treated as unoccupied, depending on the policy.
  • Vacant: A property that lacks sufficient furniture, personal property, and utility services to support human habitation (such as a home between tenants, an estate undergoing probate, or a house cleared for sale).

Some policies are stricter about vacancy than about unoccupancy; for some risks, extended unoccupancy can matter too. Ask which applies to yours.

Limits some policies set on long absences

Some policies limit or exclude certain losses if a home sits unoccupied or vacant beyond a set number of consecutive days. Some insurers use periods such as 30 or 60 days; yours may differ. Examples of what some policies address:

  • Frozen pipe and water losses: Some policies exclude water damage from frozen plumbing unless you kept the building heated or shut off and drained the water supply. Ask whether yours does and what it requires.
  • Vandalism and malicious mischief: Some policies limit this coverage after a set period of vacancy or unoccupancy. Ask whether yours does.
  • Gradual leaks: Many policies cover sudden and accidental losses and treat slow leaks differently. Ask how yours handles a leak nobody discovers for weeks.

Inspection clauses

Some policies or endorsements may require someone to check an unoccupied property on a schedule.

Depending on the specific language of your policy:

  • Your policy may set a schedule for someone to walk through the home.
  • Some policies set conditions for the heating season if the main water supply has not been closed and drained.
  • If a loss occurs and the policy had an inspection requirement that was not met, a claim may be delayed or disputed. Ask your insurer.

What to Ask Your Insurance Agent Before You Go

Before embarking on an absence of 30 days or longer, contact your insurance agent or carrier and ask these five straightforward questions. Keep a written note of the date, representative's name, and answers for your records:

  1. "How does my policy define an unoccupied home, and is there a maximum consecutive day limit before coverage terms change?"
  2. "Does my policy require someone to physically inspect the interior of the home on a specific schedule while I am away?" (If yes, ask what documentation is required if a claim occurs).
  3. "What specific precautions am I required to take regarding water and heat during winter absences?" (Confirm what you must do about water and heat to keep water damage coverage.)
  4. "If my absence extends to 60 or 90 days, do I need to purchase a temporary unoccupancy endorsement or rider?"
  5. "Does having a live-in house sitter satisfy all residency conditions under my existing policy?"

Practical Documentation Tips

If your insurer requires periodic inspections:

  • Ask your designated caretaker or neighbor to send you a quick text message after each visit (e.g., "Walked the house today, all rooms dry, thermostat set at 60°F, mail collected").
  • A dated note can help if your insurer asks how the home was checked.
