Vacancy Rules and Your Homeowner Insurance: What to Ask Before You Leave

Away Mode · Last reviewed 2026-10-16

Many homeowners assume that property insurance remains identical whether they are sleeping in their master bedroom or travelling abroad for two months. In reality, extended absences change risk profiles for insurance carriers, and standard homeowner policies contain specific conditions regarding unoccupied properties.

Understanding these conditions before an extended trip can help you maintain your coverage. Here is a clear explanation of standard policy terminology, how carriers treat unoccupied properties, and the exact questions to ask your insurance agent before departure.

Key Definitions: "Unoccupied" vs. "Vacant"

In insurance contracts, words carry specific legal definitions:

  • Unoccupied: A home that is fully furnished, has active utility services, and contains personal property, but where the residents are temporarily absent with the intention of returning. A home left empty while you take a four-week or eight-week trip is considered unoccupied.
  • Vacant: A property that lacks sufficient furniture, personal property, and utility services to support human habitation (such as a home between tenants, an estate undergoing probate, or a house cleared for sale).

While complete vacancy triggers the most severe coverage suspensions, extended unoccupancy can also affect coverage for specific risks.

The Common 30- to 60-Day Peril Exclusions

Standard homeowner policies (such as the standard HO-3 form widely used across the United States) generally maintain baseline structural coverage during temporary trips. However, policies often restrict or exclude specific types of losses if a home sits unoccupied or vacant beyond a defined consecutive timeframe—frequently 30 or 60 consecutive days:

  • Frozen Pipe and Water Losses: Most homeowner policies exclude water damage resulting from frozen plumbing unless you have exercised due diligence to maintain heat in the building (typically at or above 55°F / 13°C) or have shut off the water supply and completely drained all systems and appliances.
  • Vandalism and Malicious Mischief: Many policies suspend coverage for vandalism after 30 or 60 consecutive days of vacancy or unoccupancy, on the premise that unoccupied homes are attractive targets for unauthorized entry.
  • Gradual Leaks: Insurance is designed for sudden and accidental losses. If an interior pipe fitting leaks slowly over four weeks and rots subflooring before anyone discovers it, adjusters may scrutinize whether reasonable care was exercised to inspect the property.

Physical Inspection Clauses

In colder climates and under certain specialty policy endorsements, carriers may require that an unoccupied property undergo regular physical inspections by an adult caretaker.

Depending on the specific language of your policy:

  • Your carrier may require that someone physically enter and walk the home once every 7, 14, or 30 days.
  • Some northern policies require regular check-ins during heating season if the main water supply has not been closed and drained.
  • If a covered loss occurs and an investigation reveals the property was unvisited for months in violation of a written inspection clause, claims can be delayed or disputed.

What to Ask Your Insurance Agent Before You Go

Before embarking on an absence of 30 days or longer, contact your insurance agent or carrier and ask these five straightforward questions. Keep a written note of the date, representative's name, and answers for your records:

  1. "How does my policy define an unoccupied home, and is there a maximum consecutive day limit before coverage terms change?" (Common thresholds are 30, 60, or 90 days).
  2. "Does my policy require someone to physically inspect the interior of the home on a specific schedule while I am away?" (If yes, ask what documentation is required if a claim occurs).
  3. "What specific precautions am I required to take regarding water and heat during winter absences?" (Confirm whether turning off the main supply valve or maintaining heat at 55°F is required to preserve water damage coverage).
  4. "If my absence extends to 60 or 90 days, do I need to purchase a temporary unoccupancy endorsement or rider?" (Carriers often provide low-cost endorsements that maintain full peril coverage during multi-month sabbaticals or temporary relocations).
  5. "Does having a live-in house sitter satisfy all residency conditions under my existing policy?" (In almost all cases, having an authorized friend, family member, or sitter residing in the home keeps the property classified as occupied).

Practical Documentation Tips

If your insurer requires periodic inspections:

  • Ask your designated caretaker or neighbor to send you a quick text message after each visit (e.g., "Walked the house today, all rooms dry, thermostat set at 60°F, mail collected").
  • These timestamps provide a simple, verifiable record that routine care was maintained throughout your absence.
